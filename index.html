1. The big idea

Definition — Coulomb’s law: The electrostatic force between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charge magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

Charged objects push or pull each other without touching.

  • Same signs repel: (+ and +) or (− and −).
  • Opposite signs attract: (+ and −).
Question to the class: how strong is this force, and what does it depend on?

2. Coulomb's law

The size of the force between two point charges is:

F = k |q₁q₂|r²  where  k = 14πε₀
F : force, in newtons (N)
q₁, q₂ : charges, in coulombs (C)
r : distance between centres, in metres (m)
k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²
ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹

3. Inverse-square law: what changes the force?

If we change…The force becomes…Why
Distance × 2÷ 4F ∝ 1r², and 2² = 4
Distance × 3÷ 93² = 9
One charge × 2× 2F ∝ q₁q₂
Both charges × 2× 42 × 2 = 4
Distance has the strongest effect, because it is squared.

4. Direction and superposition

  • Size comes from the formula. Direction comes from the signs (attract or repel).
  • Newton's third law: each charge pushes or pulls the other with the same size, in opposite directions (F₁₂ = −F₂₁).
  • Superposition: with more than two charges, find each force separately, then add them as vectors.
  • On a straight line: choose a positive direction (say right). Forces to the right are +, forces to the left are −.

6. Two concept questions

Question 1. A student says: "If I double the distance between two charges, the force is halved." Is the student correct? Explain.
Show answer

No. The force depends on 1r², not 1r. Doubling r gives (2)² = 4, so the force becomes one quarter of the original (F ÷ 4), not one half.

Question 2. Charge A (+6.0 µC) pushes on charge B (+2.0 µC) with a force of 2.0 N. A student says A pushes harder because it has more charge. What is the force of B on A, and is the student right?
Show answer

The student is wrong. By Newton's third law the force of B on A is also 2.0 N, directed away from B. The two forces are an equal-and-opposite pair, even though the charges are different.

7a. Calculation problem 1: force between two charges

A +3.0 µC charge and a −5.0 µC charge are 0.50 m apart in a vacuum.
(a) Find the size and nature of the force between them.
(b) What is the force if the distance is doubled to 1.0 m?
Show full solution
  1. List the data. q₁ = +3.0 µC, q₂ = −5.0 µC, r = 0.50 m, k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². We need F.
  2. Convert units. 3.0 µC = 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ C and 5.0 µC = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ C. The distance is already in metres, so no change is needed.
  3. Choose the formula. F = k × |q₁q₂|r². The charge product is the numerator and r² is the denominator. We use the absolute value, so the minus sign does not enter the calculation. It only tells us the direction later.
  4. Multiply the charges. |q₁q₂| = (3.0 × 10⁻⁶)(5.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 15 × 10⁻¹² = 1.5 × 10⁻¹¹ C².
  5. Square the distance. r² = (0.50)² = 0.25 m².
  6. Substitute. F = (8.99 × 10⁹)(1.5 × 10⁻¹¹)0.25.
  7. Calculate the top first. (8.99 × 10⁹)(1.5 × 10⁻¹¹) = 0.13485. Then divide by 0.25: F = 0.134850.25 = 0.5394 N.
  8. Answer (a): F ≈ 0.54 N. The charges have opposite signs, so the force is attractive.
  9. Part (b): use the inverse-square law. Distance × 2 means force ÷ 4. New F = 0.53944 = 0.135 N.
  10. Check by direct calculation. With r = 1.0 m, r² = 1.0, so F = 0.134851.0 = 0.135 N. ✔ Answer (b): ≈ 0.13 N, still attractive.

7b. Calculation problem 2: net force from two charges

On a straight line: A = +4.0 µC at x = 0, C = +1.0 µC at x = 0.10 m, and B = −2.0 µC at x = 0.30 m. Find the net force on C.
A (+4.0 µC)C (+1.0 µC)B (−2.0 µC)
Show full solution
  1. Sketch and label distances. A to C is 0.10 m. C to B is 0.30 − 0.10 = 0.20 m. Take right as positive.
  2. Plan. Find the force of A on C and the force of B on C separately, then add them with their directions (superposition).
  3. Convert units. q_A = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ C, q_B = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ C (magnitude), q_C = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ C.
  4. Force of A on C. F_AC = k × |q_A q_C|r² = (8.99 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶)(1.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.10)². Top: 0.03596. Bottom: 0.01. So F_AC = 3.596 N.
  5. Direction of F_AC. A and C are both positive, so they repel. C is pushed away from A, which is to the right (+).
  6. Force of B on C. F_BC = (8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)(1.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.20)². Top: 0.01798. Bottom: 0.04. So F_BC = 0.4495 N.
  7. Direction of F_BC. B is negative and C is positive, so they attract. C is pulled towards B, which is also to the right (+).
  8. Add the forces. Both point right, so they add: F_net = 3.596 + 0.4495 = 4.0455 N.
  9. Answer: F_net ≈ 4.0 N, to the right (towards B).
  10. Sanity check. A is closer and has the larger charge, so its force should dominate. ✔ If the two forces had pointed in opposite directions, we would subtract instead of add.

8. Summary

  • F = k |q₁q₂|r². The force depends on both charges and on the square of the distance.
  • Same signs repel, opposite signs attract. The two forces are equal and opposite.
  • Always convert to coulombs and metres before substituting.
  • With several charges, find each force, then add as vectors.

Essential Exam Advice

Common Student Misconceptions & Pitfalls

Avoid losing marks on A-Level exams by recognizing these frequent mistakes.

1. Forgetting Distance Unit Conversions

Distance in Coulomb's Law must always be in meters (m). Exam questions often give separation in centimeters (cm) or millimeters (mm).
Fix: Convert cm ⇒ × 10⁻² m and mm ⇒ × 10⁻³ m before squaring!

2. Omitting the Square on Distance (r²)

A common error is to omit the square on $r$ when evaluating the expression, using $\frac{k q_1 q_2}{r}$ instead of $\frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}$.
Fix: Double check your calculator parenthesis: /(r^2).

3. Misinterpreting Negative Sign in Vectors

Plugging negative charge signs into the force formula can lead to incorrect vector directions in 2D problems.
Fix: Use the absolute value $|q_1 q_2|$ to calculate the force magnitude, then indicate the force direction on a vector diagram.

4. Confusing Electric Force (F) and Electric Field (E)

Force $F$ requires two charges ($F = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}$ in Newtons), whereas Electric Field Strength $E$ requires one source charge ($E = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$ in N C⁻¹).

5. Measuring r to the Wrong Point

Coulomb’s law uses the separation between the centres of the charges. Using the gap between surfaces gives a force that is too large.
Fix: Add the radii of spheres to any surface-to-surface gap first.

6. Mixing Up Direction and Sign

The sign of a charge tells you attract or repel. It does not tell you left or right.
Fix: Decide attract or repel first, then draw each force along the line joining the charges.

7. Leaving Charges in µC or nC

Putting 3.0 for a 3.0 µC charge makes the force 10¹² times too large.
Fix: Write every charge in coulombs (µC × 10⁻⁶, nC × 10⁻⁹) before substituting.
